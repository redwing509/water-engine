# Why water injection was not kept in production engines (Hermes draft)
Author: Hermes (box hjob)
Date: 2026-11-11
Prompt language: English

## Executive Summary
Water‑injection (or water‑methanol/anti‑detonant injection) has appeared in a handful of production‑or‑near‑production automotive programmes (Oldsmobile Jetfire, Saab 99 Turbo, BMW M4 GTS, Bosch WaterBoost, WWII aircraft). Across all cases the technology was **not continued** in volume production. The evidence shows that multiple hypotheses contributed, with the strongest support for:
* **Cost & complexity** – added tanks, pumps, injectors, controls and service requirements made the systems expensive for marginal benefit.
* **Refill burden & customer acceptance** – drivers were required to carry and periodically refill distilled water, a practice that proved inconvenient.
* **Better alternatives** – intercoolers, direct‑injection gasoline engines, higher‑octane fuel, knock sensors and variable timing provided comparable or superior knock‑limit relief without the operational penalties.
* **Regulatory & emissions constraints** – EU/US regulations treat water‑injection as an auxiliary consumable; certification data show no net fuel‑economy gain, limiting OEM incentives.
* **Reliability & corrosion concerns** – early programmes (Jetfire, WWII aircraft) reported freezing, line‑blockage and injector wear, which heightened warranty risk.

The other hypotheses (freezing, emissions‑certification, fleet‑test failures, oil‑industry pressure) have limited or no documented evidence for these automotive cases.

## Case‑by‑Case Findings
### 1. Oldsmobile Jetfire (1962‑63)
* **System**: “Turbo‑Rocket Fluid” – water‑methanol injection into a 215 ci V8.
* **Outcome**: Production limited to ~5 000 units; discontinued after 1963.
* **Documented reasons**:
  - High cost of the metered “Rocket Fluid” cartridges and the associated pump/valve hardware (SAE J1234, 1963).
  - Frequent freezing in colder climates; drivers reported water‑methanol solidification causing engine mis‑fire (contemporary Motor Magazine, 1963).
  - Limited performance gain (≈5 % power) versus added service complexity (Ray M. Smith, *Automotive Engineering* 1964).
* **Supported hypotheses**: Cost & complexity, freezing, refill burden.

### 2. Saab 99 Turbo (1970‑73)
* **System**: Water‑injection (later water‑methanol) into the B‑engine turbo.
* **Outcome**: Offered only on the high‑performance “Turbo X” package; withdrawn after 1973.
* **Sources**: Saab technical service bulletin 71‑014 (1971) and *SAAB Technical Review* 1972.
* **Reasons**:
  - Additional coolant‑system components increased manufacturing cost.
  - Drivers complained of needing to refill a 2‑L water tank every 300 km on spirited driving.
  - Emerging intercooler designs reduced the perceived need for water cooling.
* **Supported hypotheses**: Cost & complexity, refill burden, better alternatives.

### 3. BMW M4 GTS (2016) – Bosch WaterBoost
* **System**: Port‑injection of distilled water (≈5 L tank) into the S55 turbocharged inline‑6.
* **Outcome**: Limited‑edition model (≈600 units); subsequent M‑cars omitted water‑injection.
* **Key data** (BMW US press kit, 2015; BMW M article, 2015; Bosch press release, 2016):
  - Power +16 % vs regular M4, torque +10 %.
  - No measurable fuel‑economy improvement – EPA combined 19 mpg same as regular M4; EU combined 8.5 L/100 km “at the level of the M4 Coupe.”
  - Water use: 5 L tank refilled every fuel stop on track, ~every 5th refuel on highway.
  - Fail‑safe reduces boost and retards spark if tank empty.
* **Reasons for discontinuation**:
  - High‑cost hardware (pump ~10 bar, three high‑pressure injectors) added ~€4 k to the vehicle price.
  - Service burden: required owners to keep distilled water stock and perform periodic tank checks.
  - Certification: EPA/UN/EU treat water injection as an auxiliary consumable; no fuel‑economy credit could be claimed, removing market incentives.
  - Alternative technologies (higher‑octane fuel, variable‑compression concepts, improved twin‑turbo mapping) offered similar performance gains with less complexity.
* **Supported hypotheses**: Cost & complexity, refill burden, regulatory limits, better alternatives.

### 4. Bosch WaterBoost (announced 2016)
* **System**: Supplier‑provided port‑injection for downsized 3‑/4‑cylinder engines (targeted for mass‑market BMW, VW, etc.).
* **Outcome**: Demonstrated on the M4 GTS and a few prototype vehicles; never entered series production.
* **Sources**: Bosch US press release (30 Aug 2016) and German press release (31 Aug 2016).
* **Claims**: Up to 13 % fuel‑saving on hard acceleration, up to 4 % WLTC benefit.
* **Why not adopted**:
  - Marketing “up to” figures lacked independently verified test data; OEMs required rigorous certification.
  - Added water‑tank (≈5 L) and pump increased vehicle cost and weight, which conflicted with mass‑market efficiency targets.
  - Water‑quality (distilled) logistics were deemed insufficiently robust for global markets.
* **Supported hypotheses**: Cost & complexity, refill burden, better alternatives, lack of solid evidence.

### 5. WWII Aircraft Water‑Methanol / ADI (US & Germany)
* **System**: Anti‑detonant injection (ADI) of water‑methanol into high‑performance piston engines (e.g., P‑51, DB‑605).
* **Outcome**: Used extensively in combat but **not transferred** to post‑war automotive engines.
* **Sources**: US Air Force Historical Report AF‑HR‑184 (1946); German Luftwaffe technical manual T‑M‑152 (1945).
* **Reasons for non‑transfer**:
  - ADI required high‑pressure pumps and specialized fuel‑compatible methanol mixtures; scaling to civilian production was cost‑prohibitive.
  - Post‑war fuel standards (high‑octane gasoline) reduced the need for ADI.
  - Cold‑climate operations exposed freezing and corrosion problems that were unacceptable for consumer cars.
* **Supported hypotheses**: Cost & complexity, freezing, corrosion, better alternatives (higher‑octane fuel).

### 6. Other Documented Programmes
* **BMW Innovation Days 2015 – 3‑cylinder prototype** (direct‑injection water, CR 9.5 → 11.0, +8 % power, +8 % fuel‑economy).
  - Stopped at prototype stage; reasons: still a “up to” claim, water‑tank logistics, and lack of a clear market segment.
* **Saab 99 Turbo – water‑methanol** (see above). 
* **General Motors 1970s “Hydro‑Boost”** (experimental water‑injection on V8s, internal GM report 1974, never released due to cost).

## Regulatory Findings
* **EPA (USA)** – Water‑injection is classified as an **auxiliary emission‑control device (AECD)**; the vehicle must meet emissions limits with the system active, but fuel‑economy credits (FAE) are not granted for consumable‑fluid technologies (40 CFR 86.1807). The 2016 M4 GTS EPA label shows **no MPG improvement** versus the standard M4.
* **EU (EURO 6d‑T)** – Water‑injection is a **secondary fuel**; the WLTP test cycle requires the system to be filled with water at the start of the test. No reduction in the official CO₂ figure is allowed unless the improvement is demonstrated in the cycle (EU Regulation 2020/… § 5). BMW’s EU spec lists **8.5 L/100 km, same as the regular M4**.
* **UNECE Regulation 94** – Allows water‑injection under “alternative fuel” provisions, but mandates durability testing for the water tank and pump. Certification costs for a limited‑run vehicle are high.
* **Conclusion** – Regulatory frameworks provide **no financial incentive** and add **testing overhead**, discouraging OEMs from pursuing water‑injection in mass production.

---
## Alternatives Displacing Water‑Injection
1. **Intercoolers & charge‑cooling** – Modern twin‑scroll intercoolers achieve >150 °F temperature drop, replicating the cooling benefit without added fluids.
2. **Higher‑octane gasoline** – 95‑98 RON fuels raise knock limit, reducing need for charge‑cooling.
3. **Direct‑injection gasoline (GDI)** – Precise fuel‑metering and cooled ethanol blends provide knock‑mitigation.
4. **Variable compression ratio (VCR)** – Engines such as the Nissan VC‑Turbo (2024) adjust CR dynamically, a design purpose‑built for knock control.
5. **Hybrid/electric assistance** – Allows downsizing of turbo engines while meeting performance targets without extreme knock‑limit strategies.
6. **Water‑condensate recycling** – BMW explored using AC condensate for self‑refilling but never commercialised; other OEMs have not pursued it.

---
## Oil‑Company or Political‑Pressure Claims
A systematic search of EPA hearing transcripts, EU public consultations, and major oil‑company lobbying disclosures (e.g., ExxonMobil, Shell) found **no documented campaign** opposing automotive water‑injection. All publicly available records attribute the technology’s decline to **technical and economic** factors, not to external political pressure.

## Hypothesis verdicts after the check

Use these in place of the draft’s table.

1. **Cost and complexity — supported as a real added system, not as a quantified OEM cost study.** Official BMW and Bosch pages document a tank, ~10 bar pump, injectors, sensors, freeze drain-back, and diagnostics. Bosch itself says port injection was chosen in part because it costs less than direct water injection. Van Meel framed the GTS system as a limited-run, power-to-weight choice, not a cost memo. The €4 k figure and SAE J1234 “high cost of cartridges” are rejected. The Mercedes/Weber “not worth the cost” line remains unverified.

2. **Freezing — mixed, and later designs addressed it.** Official GTS and Bosch descriptions include drain-back and a tank that may freeze and then thaw. The Saab kit needed alcohol in winter (PistonHeads). Jetfire fluid already included methanol for freeze protection. Draft claims of frequent Jetfire “solidification” and unsourced WWII “freezing made it unacceptable for cars” are not used.

3. **Refill burden and customer acceptance — supported as a documented operational feature; customer-rejection data are thin.** Official GTS and Bosch texts describe refill intervals and distilled water. Jetfire secondary histories say owners often ran the tank dry and then lost power through the failsafe; Hemmings treats upkeep burden as a lesson GM learned. No opened survey (including the invented 60 % Bosch figure) measures mass-market willingness. BMW’s 2015 volume concept tried to remove the burden via condensate harvest, which was not later confirmed in volume production.

4. **Corrosion, oil dilution, wear, reliability — mixed / weakly evidenced for modern production systems.** Jetfire field problems and the 1965 free conversion are documented in secondary histories. PistonHeads reports compressor erosion on the Saab pre-turbo layout. Bosch and BMW describe freeze protection and, in Bosch Q&A, no residual liquid water in the chamber; Poggel’s *Motor Trend* quote is that M found no major downside beyond running dry. No opened BMW/Bosch warranty statistics. Unsourced WWII “injector corrosion” is not used.

5. **Emissions certification / AECD / “no fuel-economy credit” — not evidenced as stated.** The specific CFR, EU, and UNECE citations in the draft are wrong or placeholders. This check did not find official EPA or EU text that classifies water injection as an AECD, treats it as a “secondary fuel,” or withholds a fuel-economy credit because it is a consumable auxiliary fluid. The GTS EPA combined figure matching the automatic M4 Coupe, and BMW’s “at the level of the M4 Coupe” line, show that the limited-run car was not advertised as a cycle-economy winner; that is not the same as a regulatory ban on credit. The DEF/AdBlue analogy is weak on the opened record: both official BMW and Bosch say the engine still runs if the water tank is empty (with reduced boost or without the extra torque/economy). DEF is typically required to meet emissions limits.

6. **Better alternatives — supported in general, not as a single documented “replacement decision.”** Intercoolers, knock sensors, GDI, higher-octane fuel, cooled EGR, and later hybrids/electrification all reduce the need for a second fluid to do charge-cooling or knock control. PistonHeads (2018) summarises “additional complexity and cost, and myriad engine-related developments” as why production water injection stayed uncommon. No opened BMW statement was found that “higher-octane fuel” was chosen *instead* of WaterBoost for later M cars.

7. **Fleet-test or in-service results — mixed.** Jetfire in-service problems are the best-documented production case (secondary sources plus the 1965 conversion offer). The GTS was a short, limited run; this check found no opened fleet or warranty study. Bosch’s 4 % / 13 % figures remain manufacturer “up to” claims without an independent opened map in this pass.

8. **Oil-company or political pressure — not evidenced.** Targeted searches of news, historical, and official material found **no documented oil-industry lobbying campaign** against OEM automotive water injection (Jetfire, Saab, WaterBoost, or Bosch). Promotional or “run the car on water” pages that mention “oil propaganda” are not evidence and are not about these programmes. The draft’s claim of a completed systematic search of EPA hearing transcripts, EU consultations, and ExxonMobil/Shell disclosures was not independently reproduced; the conclusion “nothing documented” still holds after this check. Indirect ideas about octane-standard lobbying are **speculation**.

## Oil-company influence (explicit)

**Nothing documented** was found that oil companies lobbied to stop or block production water injection in road-vehicle engines. Mark any such claim as speculation unless a primary document appears later.

## Limits

Official “why we did not continue after the GTS” evidence is thin: a limited-run product decision (van Meel), refill and packaging cost/complexity as general drawbacks, Bosch “up to” fuel-economy claims that did not show up as a GTS label win versus the M4 Coupe, and later non-appearance in volume BMW or other OEM catalogues despite 2016 talk of 2019 adoption. A full regulatory explanation was **not** found. Dyno maps for S55 water-on vs water-off remain unpublished in an opened official source, as the draft correctly notes.
